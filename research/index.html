Research

How geometry becomes light.

Three threads — a theory, an object, an experiment — tied by one question: what does it mean for light to follow a geodesic?

01–03 Three threads Methods Ray tracing · conformal maps · single photons Anchor The Morse lens thesis, 2026 Read the thesis

On this page

01 Transformation optics 02 Geodesic lenses 03 Quantum optics 04 Open questions 05 Further reading

One sentence holds the programme together: design the geometry, and light will follow. A refractive index is a metric; a curved surface is a lens; a coordinate transformation is a material. I work on the mathematics that makes those equivalences exact — and on the numerical experiments that keep them honest.

01 / Theory

Transformation optics

Maxwell's equations are form-invariant under coordinate transformations. That single fact turns a design problem in materials into a design problem in geometry.

Start with Fermat. In a medium with refractive index $n(\mathbf{r})$, a ray makes the optical path

\[ S=\int n(\mathbf{r})\,ds \]

stationary. That is the statement that rays are geodesics of the optical metric $g_{ij}=n^{2}\delta_{ij}$. Now change coordinates, $\mathbf{r}\mapsto\mathbf{r}'$, and write the same metric in the new frame. Maxwell's equations do not notice the change; the constitutive parameters do.

If $\Lambda$ is the Jacobian of the transformation, the transformed medium is

\[ \varepsilon'=\frac{\Lambda\,\varepsilon\,\Lambda^{T}}{\det\Lambda} \]

\[ \mu'=\frac{\Lambda\,\mu\,\Lambda^{T}}{\det\Lambda}. \]

The physics is unchanged; the material has absorbed the geometry. A region of space can be compressed into a shell, and the interior becomes invisible to the field outside. This is the transformation-optics cloak, proposed independently by Pendry and by Leonhardt in 2006, and demonstrated at microwave frequencies by Schurig and co-workers the same year.

What makes the idea practical is a restriction: if the transformation is conformal in two dimensions, the required medium is isotropic. The price is that only a subset of transformations is available. Much of the field since 2006 has been an argument about that trade — anisotropy for generality, conformality for fabrication, metasurfaces for both.

My interest is the boundary between the two. A cloak that cannot be built is a statement about a metric, not a device. The useful question is not “does an equivalent medium exist?” but “what is the smallest change in the geometry that survives the tolerances of a real surface?”

Open questions

  • Bandwidth and loss: which transformations survive a dispersive, lossy medium?
  • Conformal maps in 3D: what is the analogue of the 2D Riemann mapping?
  • Inverse design: can a target ray family be turned into a manufacturable surface directly?
02 / Geometry

Geodesic lenses

A spherically symmetric index profile is a surface of revolution in disguise. The disguise is exact: rays in the medium are geodesics on the surface.

For a radially symmetric profile $n=n(r)$, the optical metric is

\[ ds^{2}=n^{2}(r)\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\varphi^{2}\right). \]

Look for a surface of revolution with profile $z(\rho)$ whose metric is the same. Matching term by term gives the three equivalence relations

\[ \rho=n(r)\,r,\qquad ds=n(r)\,dr, \]

\[ dz^{2}=ds^{2}-d\rho^{2}. \]

The first relation is the projected radius; the second identifies optical arc length with surface arc length; the third fixes the height. Differentiating $\rho=nr$ gives the master identity $s'(\rho)\,(n+rn')=n$, and the surface follows by quadrature:

\[ z(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\rho}\sqrt{s'(\xi)^{2}-1}\;d\xi. \]

The Luneburg profile makes the geometry unusually explicit. With $n^{2}=2-r^{2}$,

\[ \rho^{2}=r^{2}(2-r^{2})=1-(1-r^{2})^{2}, \]

so writing $z=1-r^{2}$ gives $\rho^{2}+z^{2}=1$. The Luneburg lens is a unit sphere. Every parallel ray focuses on the opposite pole, and every geodesic is a great circle.

The Morse family generalises this. The profile

\[ n^{2}(r)=2r^{-2+1/B}-r^{-2+2/B},\qquad 0<r\le 1, \]

reduces to Luneburg at $B=\tfrac12$ and to Eaton at $B=1$. My thesis proves that the conformal Morse lens is the $(A=B,\,C=1)$ slice of Cornbleet's general family, with $a=-1/B$, and constructs the equivalent surfaces for the intermediate profiles. The numerical ray traces agree with the surface geodesics to the discretisation error of the integrator.

There is a closure criterion hidden in the quadrature: a real surface exists only while $s'(\rho)\ge1$, and a closed lens requires the height to remain finite at the rim. The Eaton limit fails at the origin — the index diverges, the surface opens, and the retroreflector is not a closed lens. That is a geometric obstruction, not a fabrication problem.

Open questions

  • Surface tolerance: how much figure error can a geodesic lens tolerate before the caustic blurs?
  • Realisation: can a dielectric metasurface approximate the required metric?
  • Beyond revolution: which 3D metrics admit a closed, isotropic realisation?
03 / Experiment

Quantum optics

At the single-photon level, interference is not a wave passing through two slits. It is a statement about what the experiment could have told us.

Send photons through a double slit one at a time. Each photon produces one detection. After a few hundred, the detections trace an interference pattern; after a few thousand, the pattern is unmistakable. Nothing in the apparatus knows in advance where a particular photon will land.

The amplitude at the screen is the sum of the amplitudes from the two slits, and the probability is the square:

\[ I(x)\propto\left|\psi_{1}(x)+\psi_{2}(x)\right|^{2}. \]

For slit separation $d$ and screen distance $L$, the fringe spacing is $\Delta x=\lambda L/d$. The envelope is set by the slit width. The pattern is not a property of a single photon; it is a property of the ensemble, and of the fact that the two paths were indistinguishable.

Make them distinguishable. A which-path detector — a polariser, a path marker, a detector at one slit — gives the experiment information about the route. The interference term disappears, and the pattern becomes the sum of two single-slit envelopes. The visibility $V$ and the path distinguishability $D$ trade off: $V^{2}+D^{2}\le1$. This is complementarity in its quantitative form.

The teaching-lab version is less exotic than the thought experiment and more instructive. Alignment matters; a few microns of slit tilt washes out the fringes. Dark counts add a flat background. The source must be attenuated until the probability of two photons in the apparatus is small. What looks like a clean conceptual story becomes a sequence of experimental decisions — which is exactly why it is worth doing.

The figure is a simulation of that experiment, not a claim of new data. It uses $\lambda=633$ nm, $d=0.12$ mm and $L=1.2$ m; the dots are sampled from the same intensity law the apparatus would obey.

Open questions

  • What counts as a which-path measurement when the detector is a material that is itself quantum?
  • How do dark counts and finite coherence change the measured visibility?
  • Can the same geometry that shapes a classical ray also shape a single-photon wavefront?
04 / Programme

What I want to know next

Three questions that connect the threads — and that I would like to argue about with someone.

  1. Which profiles admit a closed, manufacturable surface? The closure criterion is a first filter, not a design rule. I want a classification that includes tolerance and finite aperture.
  2. Can a conformal design survive fabrication? A sphere is exact; a machined sphere is not. The sensitivity of the caustic to surface error is a well-posed numerical question.
  3. What does a which-path measurement do to the field? The textbook story is about information. A detector is also a physical object with a response time and a back-action, and I would like to understand the bridge.

If any of these is close to your work, write to me. I am especially interested in collaborations that put a computed surface into an optical test.
